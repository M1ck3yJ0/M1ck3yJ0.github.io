Far from Full Circle

The EU's goal to double circularity is, in effect, a goal to use less

In 2022, just 11.4% of the material the EU used came back into use from waste. By 2030, the EU wants that share to reach 24%, more than double. From 2004 to 2022, that share rose from 8.2% to 11.4%: each year, less than a fifth of 1% more of the material used came back. To reach 24% by 2030, about 1.6% more of it would have to come back every year. This is a story about what that goal truly asks of us.

Picture the target as a ring. The thick arc is the share of the EU's material that comes back from waste, and the thin circle is the 24% target. When the arc reaches all the way round, the target is met. In 2022, it reached less than halfway. In the middle is the new material the EU consumed that year, not counting what came back from waste: 6.4 billion tonnes, about 14 tonnes for every person, or 39 kilos a day. There are two ways to close the ring. We can recycle more, so the arc grows longer, or we can use less, so the circle grows smaller.

Recycling can only work with what comes back as waste, and much of what we use never does. Fuel is burned and gone, its carbon released into the air. Food is eaten. Stone, sand and steel become homes and roads that stand for decades. Each year, the waste that does come back weighs as much as 31% of the material the EU consumes. Yet even if all of it were recycled, the rate would reach about 25.5%, not 31%. Partly that's because a recycled tonne only partly replaces a new one, and partly because the rate weighs recycled material against all the material used, the recycled material included.

So push the first lever as far as it will go. A recycled tonne can take the place of a new one, but rarely all of it, so picture each recycled tonne replacing half a new tonne of the same material, a midpoint between replacing none and replacing all. The material in the middle shrinks a little as the arc grows. Even then, the ring closes only when the EU recycles about 93% of all its waste, the rubble, soil, mine waste and ash included: 2.3 times what it recycled in 2022. And it closes only because the EU now uses 8.1% less new material.

That rests on two hopes at once. The first is that the EU recycles nearly all of its waste, when even the European Environment Agency pictures recycling 90% only by 2050. The second is that each recycled tonne truly takes the place of a new one, and that happens only as far as markets let it. If it replaced none at all, the ring would never close this way: recycling every tonne would stop at 23.7%.

And recycling is never free. Sorting and reprocessing lose part of what is collected, and paper needs fresh fiber to keep going round. Steel scrap carries copper that can't be refined out, so the sheet for car bodies still comes almost entirely from new steel. Concrete may take only part of its stone from rubble, and in practice takes almost none: rubble goes instead under roads and into fill. And what recycling would replace is mostly sand, gravel and stone, because that is most of the waste not yet recycled. So closing the ring this way removes 8.1% of the EU's new material by weight, but only about 3.6% of the climate impact of making it, and even that is a generous estimate.

Now the other lever. Keep recycling where it was in 2022, and use less instead. To close the ring this way, the EU would need to use 59.3% less new material than it did in 2022: about 8.5 of those 14 tonnes per person. It's a great deal to ask.

As the material in the middle shrinks, the ring tightens around it, and the same recycling covers more of the ring, until the arc's two ends finally meet. This lever rests on a hope too: that the EU keeps recycling as many tonnes as it did in 2022 while using far less. Using less can also mean less waste to recycle, and if recycling slipped, the cut would have to go deeper still.

So the EU's target, which reads like a goal for recycling, is in effect a goal for using less new material. It isn't a choice between the two; we need both. But every route to 24% runs through using much less new material. If the EU recycled 70% of its waste instead of 41%, it would still need about 31% less. Recycling would stand in for about 4.5% of the EU's new material, and the EU would go without the other 26.5%. Even recycling about 93% of its waste, the EU would use at least 8% less.

The European Environment Agency came to the same conclusion. It modeled the EU recycling 70% of its waste, using 15% less material and a third less fossil fuel, all at once. Even then, the rate reached only about 22% by 2030.

The rate's biggest jumps came from using less, not recycling more

The rate's biggest jumps came from using less, not recycling more

None of this is hypothetical. Using less has already moved the EU's rate, in both directions. Each bar shows what one lever did over two years: blue for using less, or more, and gold for recycling. From 2004 to 2024, the rate rose from 8.2% to an estimated 12.2%. Recycling lifted it by 2.4% of the material used, and using less by 1.6% of it. The years the EU used less lifted it by 2.9% of the material used; the years it used more took 1.3% of it back.

The biggest jump came between 2008 and 2010, when the rate rose from 9.1% to 10.7% in two years. 84% of it came from the EU using 14% less material, most of it sand, gravel and stone. Recycling added only about a quarter of 1% of the material used. Those were the years of the financial crisis.

The latest jump came between 2022 and 2024, when the rate rose from 11.4% to an estimated 12.2%. Again, 83% of it came from using less: the EU used 6.2% less material, mostly fossil fuels. Nobody planned it to raise the rate. And part of it has already been given back: by Eurostat's first estimate, about half of the drop returned in 2025. Sand, gravel and stone recovered about three quarters of their fall. Fossil fuels kept falling.

Look at what came back and what stayed down. The dip in sand, gravel and stone had no decision behind it, and most of it returned within a year. The fall in coal, oil and gas had years of decisions behind it, and it held. For using less to last, it has to be chosen: keeping the buildings we already have, using what we make for longer, and wasting less of it. The hopeful part is that some are already choosing it.

Food shows what this means in practice. In the restaurants of IKEA stores run by Ingka, which makes about 87% of IKEA's sales, kitchen teams began weighing what they threw away, on smart scales from Winnow and Leanpath, and by 2022 they had cut their food waste by 57.8% from 2017. A better target would count what materials cost, not what they weigh

So what would help? First, saying plainly what the target asks. When the EU first set this goal, in 2020, it came in the same sentence as another: to reduce its consumption footprint, the harm our consumption does to the climate and to nature. Two years later, the EU's environment program for 2030 named, among the conditions for reaching its goals, significantly cutting both its material footprint, the tonnes it uses, and that consumption footprint, including through 2030 reduction targets where appropriate. The European Parliament has asked for those targets, and the Council has asked the Commission to assess them. In 2025, the rate got its number, 24%. The footprints are still waiting for theirs, and the coming Circular Economy Act is a chance to give it, as Austria has already done for the material it uses.

Second, counting what materials cost, not just what they weigh. Much of EU law already does. Since January 2026, importers of steel, cement and aluminum must pay for the carbon in what they bring in. Construction products will have to declare their climate impact as each product's standards are renewed, and from 2028 large new buildings must disclose theirs over their whole life. Yet the targets for circularity count tonnes, as do the EU's recycling targets for waste. Met by recycling 70% of waste and giving up the heaviest materials first, 24% would take 31% of the EU's new material away, yet cut only about 8% of the climate impact of making materials, and about 5% of what the EU imports. A number for the consumption footprint, set beside the rate, would show what those tonnes cost.

ZIN shows why this matters. Its owner kept 171,000 tonnes of concrete standing, and those tonnes never count as recycled: they never became waste, so they never came back. Only the floors that did come down, crushed into new aggregate, counted. Had the towers come down whole and been recycled, every tonne would have counted. As we read the rate, the choice that kept 21,400 tonnes of CO2 out of the air scores worse than tearing the towers down and building again. Choices like ZIN's, and like IKEA's kitchens wasting less food, are already being made, and a better measure would see them.

And most of all, making what we already have last, and designing what we make next to need less: keeping our buildings standing, using what we make for longer, wasting less of what we grow, and designing products and buildings to last, to be repaired and to use less material from the start. For homes, the International Resource Panel found that using them more intensively could cut the emissions of their materials by up to 70% by 2050 in the G7, against 14 to 18% from better recycling and 8 to 10% from designs that use less material.

Recycling could close the ring alone only by reaching almost all our waste, and using less of everything would take a cut far deeper than any the EU has made. We will need both, and a target that rewards the cuts that matter most.

Method

When recycling rises, we assume each extra recycled tonne replaces half a new tonne of the same material. This is an assumed midpoint, not an estimate. How much new material a recycled tonne displaces is set by markets, and it can be close to none (Zink, Geyer and Startz 2018). The two ends are recycled tonnes simply adding to new ones, as in the European Environment Agency's 2030 scenarios (ETC CE Report 2023/6), and each replacing a whole new tonne, which ETC CE Report 2025/3 calls an upper limit. How much new material has to go to reach 24% at a given level of recycling is the same under all three, so the 59.3% and 31.0% hold either way. What changes is how far recycling alone gets, and how much of a cut recycling stands in for.

Replacement could also fall below none: a recycled tonne can end up adding to new production (Zink, Geyer and Startz 2018). For scale only, one model of the copper market finds that a lasting rise in scrap displaces about half a tonne of mined copper per tonne (Ryter et al. 2022). That is one metal, and not the basis for our midpoint.

When the EU uses less new material, we hold its waste and recycling at 2022's tonnes. The European Environment Agency's own scenarios for the doubling do the same, cutting material use while keeping recycled material constant (ETC CE Report 2023/6). The record supports it over a few years. In the five two-year steps since 2004 when the EU used less material, recycled material held or rose in three. From 2008 to 2010, material use fell 14.0% while recycled material rose 3.0%. In the other two it fell, by 0.6% from 2012 to 2014 and by 7.6% from 2018 to 2020 (our calculation from Eurostat). Most of the waste that comes back, rubble above all, comes from buildings and goods put in place years or decades earlier, so it doesn't fall with this year's use (ETC CE Report 2023/6).

Over longer spans, what comes back follows what goes in more closely. Growing stocks are what keep the loop open, and stocks that stopped growing would make closed loops more possible (Krausmann et al. 2017). In the United States, stocks held near today's level would give a steady flow of waste back after 2050 (Streeck et al. 2021). Once stocks stop growing, a lasting cut in use would in time bring less waste back too. Neither paper models that, so it is our reading. Some cuts shorten the arc sooner: burning less fuel and biomass for energy, or wasting less food, leaves less to recycle (ETC CE Report 2023/6).

This has limits. The largest falls on record are 14.0% in two years, from 2008 to 2010, and 6.2%, from 2022 to 2024 (estimated), far from 59.3%. A cut of 59.3% with all of 2022's waste still coming back would have to come wholly from material that doesn't come back as waste, such as fuel burned, food eaten and what is added to buildings and infrastructure, and it would take 85.7% of it. So 59.3% is a floor: if recycling slipped as use fell, the cut needed would go deeper. The model also assumes nothing else replaces the material that is cut, and it treats all minerals alike.

Using less lifts the rate because the rate is recycled material over all material used, recycled material included. In 2022 the EU used 0.13 t of recycled material for every tonne of new material. A tonne not used lifts the rate only if it takes less than that with it. With recycling held, it takes none. If recycling followed use down, a tonne of fossil fuels would take about 0.03 t with it and a tonne of biomass about 0.10 t, so cutting them would still lift the rate, while a tonne of minerals would take about 0.15 t and a tonne of metal ores about 0.25 t, so cutting them would lower it (env_wassd).

Doubling the rate has three figures. The European Environment Agency doubles 2020's rate: 11.7% to 23.4% in 2023 (EEA 2023), and 22.4% on its indicator page, from 2020's rate as since revised to 11.2%. The Clean Industrial Deal aims for 24%, "from 11.8% today", which the Commission calls doubling a rate of about 12% and the EEA a slight increase on the doubling. 24% is the figure used here.

Eurostat counts as recycled only waste recycled into materials (operations R2 to R11), not waste burned for energy or used as backfill, from its waste treatment statistics (env_wastrt), net of waste traded across borders for recycling (env_ac_cur metadata, sections 3.1, 3.4 and 18.1). Rebuilt from those tonnes, before trade, 2022's rate comes to 11.2%, against 11.4% published, possibly because the two downloads come from different releases; with backfilling counted, it would be 14.6%. To split by material we use env_wassd, which counts less waste (1,675 Mt treated and 741 Mt recycled, against env_wastrt's 1,971 Mt and 809 Mt), so its material shares are scaled to the rate's totals. Other products and waste for final treatment, 0.5% of EU material use in 2022, are left out of the shards.

Recycling every tonne has never been enough to go far past a quarter. In every reporting year from 2010 to 2022, recycling all of the EU's treated waste would have lifted the rate to between 25.5% and 28.1%, with each extra recycled tonne replacing half a new one. If recycled tonnes replaced none, it would have been 23.7% to 25.8%; if each replaced a whole new tonne, 27.5% to 30.9% (our calculation from Eurostat). The 59.3% less new material that closes the ring by using less alone is also our arithmetic, not a published figure; the Method note sets out what it assumes.

When recycling rises, as in panels 4 and 6, the new tonnes it replaces are split across materials by the waste not yet recycled: 86.5% minerals, 9.5% biomass, 3.2% fossil fuels and 0.9% metal ores (env_wassd). Sorted by Eurostat's waste categories instead, 75.8% of it is major mineral waste, about 82% with ash and mineral residues (env_wastrt). Those tonnes leave a disc whose shards cover each group's share of the EU's material consumption: 52.0% minerals, 23.3% biomass, 19.3% fossil fuels and 5.5% metal ores (env_ac_mfa).

The climate impact removed is each group's share of its own consumption removed, times its share of the climate impact of making the EU's materials (ETC CE Report 2025/3, Figure 3-4). For the tonnes recycling replaces, that uses each group's average impact per tonne, which flatters recycling: recycled aggregate stands in for natural sand, gravel and crushed stone, not for cement, and concrete made with it often needs more cement (JRC 2023). Nor does it count the energy recycling itself uses. So the 3.6% of climate impact removed when recycling alone closes the ring is generous.

Imports are each group's imports from outside the EU in 2022 (env_ac_mfa). They were 70.9% of the fossil fuels and 50.3% of the metal ores entering the EU's economy, against 12.2% of its biomass and 3.2% of its minerals. Any route's cut in imports assumes each group's imports fall in proportion to its use.

Following Eurostat's method, waste the EU exports for recycling counts as circular and waste it imports for recycling is deducted. In 2022, across the four groups, it exported 36.4 million tonnes and imported 40.7 million (env_ac_sd), so trade lowered its circular use slightly. Metals ran the other way: 19.5 million tonnes went out against 5.6 million in, and exports were 19.5% of metals' circular use. Because env_wassd's tonnes are scaled to the rate's totals (Method), these splits by material are approximate.

The rate's biggest jumps came from using less, not recycling more

Source: Eurostat. Split by lever: our calculation.

Each two-year change in the rate is split into what recycling did and what using less or more did (our calculation). The rate is circular use over circular use plus material consumption, so circular use is worked back from the published rate (env_ac_cur) and consumption (env_ac_mfa). Recycling's part of a step is the change that circular use alone would have made, averaged over holding consumption at its starting and at its ending level; using less or more is the rest, so the two add up to the change. Over the whole span, as the rate rose from 8.2% in 2004 to 12.2% in 2024, recycling accounts for about 60% of the rise and using less for about 40%. In the steps when the EU used less, that lifted the rate more than twice as much as the steps of using more pulled it down.

The rate is published to one decimal, and circular use worked back from it carries that rounding. So a step's recycling part can be off by as much as 0.1% of material used either way, which weighs most on the smallest bars and in the country explorer. We keep the published rate rather than a rebuild from tonnes, which gives a slightly lower 2022 rate (Method).

The last step, 2022 to 2024, rests on Eurostat's estimates of the 2024 rate and material use, and is marked as an estimate. For 2025 there is no rate yet, only Eurostat's imputed material use. By it, the EU used 3.4% more material in 2025, about half (51.9%) of what it had cut from 2022 to 2024. Minerals made up 64.2% of the rise and recovered 77.6% of their fall, while fossil fuels fell a further 1.8%.

Each country's ring runs on the story's model (Method), with its own figures for 2022. Recycling rises to 70% of the waste the country treats, the European Environment Agency's scenario for the EU in 2030 (EEA 2023); Italy, which already recycles 85.6%, stays where it is. Each extra recycled tonne replaces half a new one, an assumed midpoint, and the tonnes it replaces leave the disc by the country's waste not yet recycled, as in the ring's note. The cut still needed then takes the same share of every material. For a country still short of 24% after recycling, how much new material has to go is the same whatever recycling replaces; only the split between what recycling stands in for and what the country goes without changes. Tonnes per person are material consumption per person (env_ac_mfa).

At the midpoint, 19 of the 27 stay short of 24% with 70% of their waste recycled, as they do if each recycled tonne replaced a whole new one; if recycled tonnes replaced none, 23 would. The eight that pass are Austria, Bulgaria, Estonia, Finland, Luxembourg, Malta, the Netherlands and Sweden, Malta only just, at 24.1%. Bulgaria, Estonia, the Netherlands and Sweden would pass even if recycled tonnes replaced none (our calculation from Eurostat).

What sets the size of a country's cut is how much waste it treats for each tonne of material it consumes: 30.9% for the EU, from 5.7% in Latvia to 68.5% in the Netherlands. Treated waste is the waste treated in the country, so it can include waste brought in from abroad. Luxembourg treated 10.1 million tonnes in 2022 (env_wastrt), of which it had brought in 3.1 million to recycle (env_ac_sd), against 15.3 million tonnes of material consumed.

To split a country's waste by material, we scale env_wassd's tonnes to the rate's totals, as for the EU. For 15 countries that split needs care. Bulgaria, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania and Malta don't publish their trade in waste by material, and for the rest env_wassd's tonnes are more than half off the rate's: Austria, Belgium, Croatia, Cyprus, Ireland, Italy, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Latvia, the Netherlands, Spain and Sweden. In Ireland (biomass) and Cyprus (fossil fuels), one material comes out with negative circular use, because more of it was brought in to be recycled than was recycled. The split decides only which shards leave the disc. The card gives the split between recycling and going without in total, which doesn't depend on it.

Five countries carry a warning. Eurostat imputed Romania's material use for 2009 to 2019, and the minerals it reported then rose 42% from 2018 to 2020, from 314 to 445 million tonnes (env_ac_mfa). The waste Sweden recycled doubled from 2020 to 2022, from 17.2 to 33.9 million tonnes, while its recycling outside mineral wastes held at 7.2 to 7.8 million; the categories of its major mineral wastes are confidential for 2022 (env_wastrt). Estonia's, Finland's and Bulgaria's rates swing widely from one report to the next on recycling alone, much of it oil shale mining rock in Estonia, and soil and rock in Finland.

Germany's line rests on its National Circular Economy Strategy, adopted in December 2024, which pairs doubling its rate by 2030 with significantly reducing the primary raw material it uses by 2045 (BMUV, English summary). That aim is measured as raw material consumption, a footprint, not the material consumed in the ring, and the strategy gives no number for Germany.

The country waterfall splits each two-year step as the EU's does (the waterfall's note), from 2010, the first year Eurostat gives countries' rates, to 2024. Eurostat imputed the 2024 rate for 26 of the 27, all but Luxembourg, before 2024's waste figures were published, so the last step is marked as an estimate for every country. The rate is published to one decimal, and that rounding weighs most on a small country's bars. Latvia's recycling arrow from 2012 to 2014 is labeled as waste brought in: the rate subtracts waste brought in from abroad to be recycled, since it counts for the country that sent it, and Latvia brought in much less.

Your turn: close the EU's ring

Sources: Eurostat; climate shares and total, ETC CE Report 2025/3; recycling scenarios, EEA. EU, 2022. Removals: our calculation.

The game runs on the same model as the story. Waste is held at 2022's 1,971 million tonnes treated. The knob starts at the 41.1% of it recycled in 2022 by the rate's own count (Eurostat's waste statistics give 40.8%), and each extra recycled tonne replaces half a new one, an assumed midpoint (Method), split across materials by the waste not yet recycled, as in the ring's note. A tap takes 5.5% of 2022's new material from one material. Recycling replaces first, and taps cut only what it hasn't, so a cut gives way as recycling rises. Its endings match the story: 59.3% less new material at 2022's recycling, 31.0% at 70%, and recycling alone closing the ring between 93% and 94% of waste recycled.

Climate impact removed is each material's share removed of its own consumption, times its share of the climate impact of making the EU's materials (ETC CE Report 2025/3, Figure 3-4), applied to Eurostat's tonnes. As in the ring's note, the credit for recycling's replaced tonnes uses average impact per tonne, which flatters recycling. The tonnes of CO2e are that share of 2.89 billion tonnes, the climate impact of the raw materials used in the EU in 2022 (ETC CE Report 2025/3, Table 3-1). That is a footprint of the EU's final demand, measured to the factory gate, so it can't be set beside the EU's own emissions. The report doesn't say Figure 3-4's shares come from the same calculation as the total, though both rest on the same models (FIGARO, 2024 edition, and EXIOBASE 3.8.2).

Imports removed are measured against the 1.66 billion tonnes the EU imported from outside it in 2022 (env_ac_mfa), and assume each material's imports fall in proportion to its use. A material with nothing left counts as all removed.

A better target would count what materials cost, not what they weigh

Sources: European Commission; Decision (EU) 2022/591; European Parliament; Council of the EU; European Environment Agency; Austria's Circular Economy Strategy; Regulation (EU) 2023/956; Regulation (EU) 2024/3110; Directive (EU) 2024/1275; Regulation (EU) 2024/1252; European Court of Auditors; Directive 2008/98/EC; Haupt and Hellweg 2019; EPA Research Report 458; Eurostat; ETC CE Report 2025/3; International Resource Panel; Krausmann et al. 2017. Climate impact and imports removed: our calculation.

The 2020 Circular Economy Action Plan set the doubling in the same sentence as a pledge to reduce the EU's consumption footprint (COM(2020) 98, Introduction). The Clean Industrial Deal of 2025 made 24% by 2030 one of its targets, citing that plan, and gave as its reasons making the most of limited resources, depending less on others, resilience, lower production costs and less CO2 (COM(2025) 85, sections 1 and 3). It never mentions using less material: "footprint" appears once, for labels showing consumers a product's carbon footprint, and "consumption" only for energy (searched in the EUR-Lex text). The Commission plans to adopt the Circular Economy Act in the fourth quarter of 2026; trade press expects it on December 2, and one report says 2027.

The 8th Environment Action Programme lists, among the conditions its objectives "shall require" of the Commission, member states and others "as appropriate," "significantly decreasing the Union's material and consumption footprints to bring them into planetary boundaries as soon as possible, including through the introduction of Union 2030 reduction targets, as appropriate" (Decision (EU) 2022/591, Article 3(s)). The material footprint is the raw material, in tonnes, needed to make what the EU consumes; the consumption footprint is the environmental impact of that consumption, scored by the Joint Research Centre in 16 impact categories, climate change among them. The Commission's monitoring targets for both are only to "significantly decrease" them, while most of the targets beside them carry a number, the rate's doubling among them (COM(2022) 357). The European Parliament asked for binding 2030 targets for both footprints (resolution of February 10, 2021), and the Council invited the Commission to assess "ambitious and economically feasible science-based targets" for them (conclusions of June 17, 2024, paragraph 28(c)). The European Environment Agency says circular policies should extend beyond waste "to possibly include targets on resource use or material footprint" (EEA 2024, executive summary). Austria's strategy pairs an 18% rate target for 2030 with bringing material use down to 14 tonnes per person by 2030.

At 70% of waste recycled, the EU still needs 31.0% less new material: recycling replaces 4.5%, mostly minerals, and it goes without the other 26.5% (Method). If what it goes without is taken whole material by whole material, heaviest first, that is minerals alone, and the cut removes 7.8% of the climate impact of making the EU's materials and 4.6% of what it imports from outside the EU. Taken by climate impact per tonne instead, metal ores first and then biomass, the same tonnes remove 59.5% of the climate impact and 26.4% of imports. Both are counted as in the game's note, with the same credit to recycling.

Much of EU law already counts the carbon in materials. Since January 1, 2026, importers of cement, iron and steel, aluminum, fertilizers, electricity and hydrogen owe certificates for the greenhouse gases embedded in what they import, priced on the EU Emissions Trading System (Regulation (EU) 2023/956). Construction products' declarations of performance must cover their climate change effects over their life cycle from January 8, 2026, but only once a product family's harmonized standard has been renewed under the new regulation, and the old standards may stay in use until 2040 (Regulation (EU) 2024/3110, Article 15(3) and Annex II). From January 1, 2028, new buildings over 1,000 square meters must have their carbon over their whole life calculated and disclosed in their energy performance certificate, and all new buildings from 2030 (Directive (EU) 2024/1275). The Critical Raw Materials Act asks the Commission and member states to "moderate the expected increase" in the EU's use of critical raw materials, through technology and resource efficiency. For 17 strategic raw materials, it sets 2030 benchmarks for the EU's own capacity, among them recycling at least 25% of its yearly use, and by January 1, 2027, the Commission must set recycling benchmarks for each, as a share of what is in the relevant waste (Regulation (EU) 2024/1252, Article 5 and Annex I). The distance is long: of the 26 critical raw materials the EU needs for the energy transition, 10 aren't recycled at all and 7 only at 1 to 5% (European Court of Auditors, Special Report 04/2026).The EU's waste targets, by contrast, are set by weight, such as at least 70% by weight of non-hazardous construction and demolition waste reused, recycled or otherwise recovered, backfilling included (Directive 2008/98/EC, Article 11(2)). That a measure by weight misses what materials cost is published: mass-based indicators such as recycling rates "fail to cover the environmental perspective" (Haupt and Hellweg 2019), the rate "does not directly consider other environmental pressures such as greenhouse gas emissions" (EPA Research Report 458), and a higher rate "does not necessarily lead to lower environmental impacts" (ETC CE Report 2025/3).

That ZIN scores worse than demolition is our reading of Eurostat's method, not a published finding. Published work says only that the rate leaves out strategies like making things last, and reads longer lifetimes as lifting it by cutting new material, which holds against building new but not against tearing down and rebuilding. Rubble crushed into aggregate counts as recycling (operation R5) and so as circular use, while rubble used to fill in excavations doesn't (env_ac_cur metadata). Demolishing and rebuilding adds both recycled rubble and new material to what is used. It lifts the rate whenever the rubble recycled is a larger share of that extra material than the rate already is, 11.4% for the EU, which holds for a building that is mostly concrete. Keeping it standing adds neither. The 30,000 tonnes of rubble from ZIN's demolished floors, crushed into certified aggregate, count as recycling under the same rule. One building barely moves the EU's rate: the point is the direction it moves, not how far. ZIN's tonnes and carbon are set out in its note.

The remedy of getting more from what is already built, by using it more intensively, making it last longer and designing it better, is Krausmann et al. 2017's. The figures for homes are the International Resource Panel's, for the G7 in 2050, and count the emissions of the materials homes need: up to 70% less from using homes more intensively, 14 to 18% from better recycling of construction materials and 8 to 10% from designs that use less material (IRP 2020, factsheet). The EEA likewise calls for more intensity of use per product and much longer product lifetimes, and for putting the reduction of resource use first (EEA 2024, executive summary). Recycling closing the ring alone only by reaching almost all our waste is the 93.4% of waste recycled in the Method table, and the cut far deeper than any the EU has made is the 59.3% needed with recycling held at 2022, against the largest two-year fall on record, 14.0% (Method).
