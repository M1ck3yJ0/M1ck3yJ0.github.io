Far from Full Circle

The EU's goal to double circularity is, in effect, a goal to use less

In 2022, just 11.4% of the material the EU used came back into use from waste. By 2030, the EU wants that share to reach 24%, more than double. Over the past twenty years, it has risen by about a fifth of a point a year. Getting there now would take about 1.6 points a year. This is a story about what that goal truly asks of us.

Picture the target as a ring. The thick arc is the share of the EU's material that comes back from waste, and the thin circle is the 24% target. When the arc reaches all the way round, the target is met. In 2022, it reached less than halfway. In the middle is the material the EU consumed that year: 6.4 billion tonnes, about 14 tonnes for every person, or 39 kilos a day. There are two ways to close the ring. We can recycle more, so the arc grows longer, or we can use less, so the circle grows smaller.

Recycling can only work with what comes back as waste, and much of what we use never does. Fuel is burned and gone, its carbon released into the air. Food is eaten. Stone, sand and steel become homes and roads that stand for decades. Each year, the waste that does come back weighs as much as 31% of the material the EU consumes. And recycling it lengthens the arc by less than you might expect. Recycled material counts as material used too, so every tonne recycled also adds to the total it's measured against.

So imagine the EU recycled every tonne that comes back, the rubble, soil, stone and ash included. The arc would reach 99% of the way round, a rate of 23.7%, and still fall just short. Recycling all of it isn't practical, either. Even perfect recycling, on its own, can't close the ring.

Now the other lever. Keep recycling where it was in 2022, and use less instead. To close the ring this way, the EU would need to use 59.3% less material than it did in 2022: about 8.5 of those 14 tonnes per person. It's a great deal to ask.

As the material in the middle shrinks, the ring tightens around it, and the same recycling covers more of the ring, until the arc's two ends finally meet. Using less is the only lever that can close the ring on its own.

So the EU's target, which reads like a goal for recycling, is in effect a goal for using less. It isn't a choice between the two; we need both. But there's no realistic route to 24% that doesn't run through using much less. Even if the EU recycled 70% of its waste instead of 41%, it would still need to use about 31% less material.

The European Environment Agency came to the same conclusion. It modeled the EU recycling 70% of its waste, using 15% less material and a third less fossil fuel, all at once. Even then, the rate reached only about 22% by 2030.

None of this is hypothetical. Using less has already moved the EU's rate, in both directions. Each bar shows what one lever did over two years: blue for using less, or more, and gold for recycling. From 2004 to 2024, the rate rose 4 points, 2.4 of them from recycling and 1.6 from using less. In the years the EU used less, the rate gained 2.9 points. In the years it used more, it gave 1.3 back.

The biggest jump came between 2008 and 2010, when the rate rose 1.6 points in two years. 84% of it came from the EU using 14% less material, most of it sand, gravel and stone. Recycling added only about a quarter of a point. Those were the years of the financial crisis.

The latest jump came between 2022 and 2024, when the rate rose 0.8 points. Again, 83% of it came from using less: the EU used 6.2% less material, mostly fossil fuels. Nobody planned it to raise the rate. And part of it has already been given back: by Eurostat's first estimate, about half of the drop returned in 2025. Sand, gravel and stone recovered about three quarters of their fall. Fossil fuels kept falling.

Look at what came back and what stayed down. The dip in sand, gravel and stone had no decision behind it, and most of it returned within a year. The fall in coal, oil and gas had years of decisions behind it, and it held. For using less to last, it has to be chosen: keeping the buildings we already have, using what we make for longer, and wasting less of it. The hopeful part is that some are already choosing it.

In Brussels, the old World Trade Center towers weren't torn down. Their owner kept 85% of the structure, 171,000 tonnes of concrete, and built ZIN around it. Compared with an efficient new building, that halved the structure's upfront carbon and cut the whole building's by a third: 21,400 tonnes of CO2 that were never emitted. The 30,000 tonnes that did come down became new aggregate within 15 km. For every tonne recycled, almost six stayed standing, and those never had to be made again.

There's something the rate can't see. It counts materials by weight, and only by weight, so a tonne of gravel counts the same as a tonne of metal or a tonne of food. But weight isn't impact. Here is everything the EU used in 2022, by weight, in four kinds of material.

Now follow each material across, from its share of the weight to its share of the climate impact of making it.

Sand, gravel, stone and other minerals are more than half of the EU's material use by weight, 52.3%, yet only 11.4% of the climate impact of making materials. The tonnes that dominate the rate do the least harm to the climate, tonne for tonne. They are also the tonnes Europe relies on no one for: about 3% come from outside the EU.

Biomass, the crops, feed and wood we grow and harvest, runs the other way. It's 22.7% of the weight, but 45.7% of the climate impact, the largest share of any of the four.

Food shows what this means in practice. In the restaurants of IKEA stores run by Ingka, which makes about 87% of IKEA's sales, kitchen teams began weighing what they threw away, on smart scales from Winnow and Leanpath, and by 2022 they had cut their food waste by 57.8% from 2017. That saved more than 20 million meals over four years, and 36,000 tonnes of CO2e. By 2024, the cut had reached 60.5%. It saves money too: Winnow says most commercial kitchens waste 5 to 15% of the food they buy, so halving that trims the food bill by 2.5 to 7.5%. Ingka has held a stake in Winnow since 2019.

Side by side, the two aren't like for like. ZIN's figure is CO2 for its construction stage only, saved once, while IKEA's kitchens' figure is CO2e from about 400 restaurants over four years. So this isn't about which did more. It's about how much carbon a tonne carries, depending on the material. By weight, the rate's only measure, ZIN's 171,000 tonnes of concrete kept standing are 95% of what the two saved, against an estimated 8,900 tonnes of food. By carbon, the kitchens' 5% becomes 63%. Each tonne of concrete kept standing saved an eighth of a tonne of CO2; each tonne of food kept out of the bin, about four tonnes of CO2e, roughly 30 times as much.

So what would help? First, honesty in the target. When the EU first set this goal, in 2020, it came in the same sentence as another: to reduce its consumption footprint. In 2025, its industrial plan put a number on the rate, 24%, and left the footprint out. It would help to say plainly that this goal, and any higher one after 2030, is a goal to use less. Austria already has: alongside its 18% rate target for 2030, it aims to bring material use down to 14 tonnes per person. The EU's coming Circular Economy Act is a chance to do the same.

Second, counting what materials cost, not just what they weigh. A 24% rate met by giving up the heaviest materials first would cut only about a quarter of the climate impact of making materials, and about a tenth of the tonnes the EU imports. Some rules already look past weight: from 2028, large new buildings in the EU must disclose their carbon over their whole life, and every new building from 2030. In our own reports, we can do the same, counting the tonnes we avoid beside the tonnes we recycle, and measuring both by what they cost the climate.

And most of all, making what we already have last: keeping our buildings standing, using what we make for longer, wasting less of what we grow. For homes, the International Resource Panel found that using them more intensively could cut the emissions of their materials by up to 70% by 2050 in the G7, against 14 to 18% from better recycling. ZIN didn't wait for a target, and neither did IKEA's kitchens. We don't have to either. The ring won't close by recycling alone. It closes when we choose to need less.
