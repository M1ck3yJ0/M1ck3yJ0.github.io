Far from Full Circle

To double its circularity by 2030, the EU needs about a third less new material, even recycling far more

By Milcah M. Joseph · [launch date] · Eurostat data

This is a journey that began with my work on sustainability data in Ireland. I didn't always live here, so the waste management efforts in Europe have always been impressive to me. So the 2% circularity rate for Ireland, one of the lowest in the EU, came out of left field, and I followed a very long rabbit hole trying to understand it. But the longer I spent with the numbers, the more I found myself wondering what they were actually telling us. If the point of becoming more circular is to take less from the earth, to do less harm to the life it sustains and to leave behind a world worth living in, how well are we measuring that? So I turned to the EU as a whole and the target it has set for 2030.

The newest figure for the EU's circularity rate (the share of all the material we use that comes from recycling) is Eurostat's estimate for 2024: 12.2%, against a target of 24% by 2030. To get there, the rate would have to climb by about 2% of all the material we use each year, more than it climbed in all fourteen years from 2010 to 2024, when it went from 10.7% to 12.2%.

The European Environment Agency has warned since 2023 that recycling more won't get there on its own, and in 2025 its European Topic Centre worked out how much less material the EU would have to use even with recycling pushed close to its limit. What I wanted to see was how far each lever would have to go on its own, how much that depends on what a recycled tonne really replaces, and what it would mean for each EU country, before looking at what the target can't see, such as a building in Brussels that could have done more for the rate torn down than kept standing. The model behind the ring and all its calculations use 2022, the latest year for which Eurostat has reported everything the rate is made of.

Not everything we use can come back: fuel is burned and its carbon goes into the air, and food is eaten. Each year the EU treats waste weighing as much as 31% of the material it consumes, but it isn't that year's material coming back. Most of it is soil, rubble and the rock left over from mining: the rubble comes from buildings put up decades ago, and much of the soil and rock never counted as material used at all. Much of this waste is never recycled, and most of what isn't goes back into the ground, buried in landfills or used to fill land that has been dug out.

This was the second thing that shocked me about Ireland: most of its waste is soil and rock, and most of what it doesn't recycle is used as fill, which the rate doesn't count as recycling.

Before the ring moves, try a guess: to close it by recycling alone, how much of its waste would the EU have to recycle?

The answer is almost all of it, so watch the ring as recycling climbs. The thick gold arc is the share of the EU's material that comes from recycling, and the thin circle is the 24% target. The disc in the middle is the material the EU consumes, what it extracts plus what it imports minus what it exports: 6.4 billion tonnes in 2022, about 14 tonnes for every person. I call it new material for short, though some imported goods already contain recycled material. As recycling rises, the arc grows and the disc shrinks a little, because I assume each recycled tonne replaces half a new one, a midpoint between replacing none and replacing all. On that assumption the ring closes only when the EU recycles about 93% of all its waste, the soil, rubble, mine waste and ash included: 2.3 times what it recycled in 2022, and by then it uses 8.1% less new material. If each recycled tonne replaced a whole new one, it would take about 87%. If each replaced none, recycling every tonne would stop at 23.7%, and the ring would never close this way.

Recycling every last tonne would take the rate only to about 25.5%, not the 31% that comes back, because a recycled tonne only partly replaces a new one, and recycled material counts as material used too.

Closing it this way rests on two hopes. One is that recycling reaches nearly all of the EU's waste, the soil, rubble, mine waste and ash included, when even the European Environment Agency pictures recycling 90% only by 2050. The other is that each recycled tonne really does take the place of half a new one, which happens only as far as markets let it.

In practice, recycled material doesn't fully replace new material either. Sorting and reprocessing lose part of what is collected, and paper needs fresh fiber to keep going round. Steel scrap carries copper that can't be refined out, so the sheet for car bodies still comes almost entirely from new steel. Concrete may take only part of its stone from rubble, and in practice takes almost none: rubble goes instead under roads and into fill. What recycling would replace is also mostly sand, gravel and stone, since that is most of the waste not yet recycled, so closing the ring this way removes 8.1% of the EU's new material by weight but only about 3.6% of the climate cost of producing it, burning fuels not counted: a rough estimate, and a generous one to recycling.

Recycling can only work with what has already been taken from the ground and the land, while the other lever, using less, asks how much of it we needed to take at all. If recycling stayed where it was in 2022, closing the ring would take 59.3% less new material than the EU used that year, about 8.5 of those 14 tonnes per person, which is a great deal to ask.

As the disc shrinks, the ring tightens around it and the same recycling covers more of it, until the arc's two ends meet. This route also rests on a hope, that the EU keeps recycling as many tonnes as it did in 2022 while using far less. Using less can mean less waste to recycle. If recycling fell in step with use, cutting fuels or biomass would still lift the rate, but cutting minerals or metal ores would lower it, because each of their tonnes brings more recycled material with it than the EU's average. Wherever recycling slipped, the cut would have to be deeper than 59.3%.

So the target, which reads like a goal for recycling, is a goal for using less. One number holds whatever a recycled tonne replaces: if the EU recycled 70% of its waste instead of 41%, it would still need 31% less new material to reach 24%. With each recycled tonne replacing half a new one, recycling stands in for only about 4.5% of that cut, and nothing stands in for the rest. Country by country, 19 of the EU's 27 would still have to use less.

The European Environment Agency came to the same conclusion when it modeled the EU recycling 70% of its waste, using 15% less material and a third less fossil fuel, all at once: the rate still reached only about 22% by 2030. In 2025 its European Topic Centre worked out what doubling would take: even with fossil fuels cut by a third, recycling at the most it judged possible, about 69% of all waste, and each recycled tonne replacing a whole new one, the EU would have to consume 26% less material than in 2020.

Recycling lifted the rate slowly; its biggest jump came from using less, in a crisis

From 2004 to 2024, the EU's rate rose from 8.2% to an estimated 12.2%. The shaded years mark three shocks, the financial crisis, COVID and the energy crisis, and the rate rose fastest in the financial crisis, fell during COVID and rose again in the energy crisis.

To see what moved it, I split each two-year step in two: a blue bar for what using less, or more, did to the rate, and a gold bar for what recycling did. Over those twenty years, recycling lifted the rate by 2.4% of the material used and using less by 1.6% of it. That 1.6% is the 2.9% gained in the years the EU used less, minus the 1.3% lost in the years it used more. Around COVID the EU used less but also recycled less, so the rate fell.

The biggest jump came between 2008 and 2010, the years of the financial crisis, when the rate rose from 9.1% to 10.7%. 84% of the rise came from the EU using 14% less material, most of it sand, gravel and stone, while recycling added only about a quarter of 1% of the material used. That jump came with a cost: the same crisis left about 6 million fewer people in work across the EU by 2010, about 1.7 million of them in construction.

The latest jump came between 2022 and 2024, when the rate rose from 11.4% to an estimated 12.2%. 83% of it again came from using less: in the years of the energy crisis, the EU used 6.2% less material, mostly fossil fuels, though nobody planned that to raise the rate. Part of it has already been given back. By Eurostat's first estimate, about half of the drop returned in 2025, as sand, gravel and stone recovered about three quarters of their fall while fossil fuels kept falling.

That was using less by crisis. Can we use less by choice, and keep what makes life good?

Using less coal, oil and gas followed years of decisions, with the energy crisis on top of them, and that fall has held.

Keeping a building standing cut its upfront carbon by a third; tearing it down could have scored better

Keeping a building standing cut its upfront carbon by a third; tearing it down could have scored better

Some are already choosing to use less, in ways that last. In Brussels, the owner of the old World Trade Center towers kept their cores and basements, about 85% of the structure by weight, or 171,000 tonnes, and built The World Trade Center towers before, and ZIN after. [Photo credit] around them. Compared with an efficient new building, that halved the structure's upfront carbon and cut the whole building's by a third: by the engineers' estimate, 21,400 tonnes of CO2 that were never emitted. That carbon would have added to the warming Europe is already living with: in the summer of 2026, an estimated 32,000 deaths in its cities came from heat that climate change added. The 30,000 tonnes that did come down were crushed into aggregate, and at least 3,500 tonnes of it went into ZIN's own concrete, made within 15 kilometers of the site. So for every tonne that came down, almost six stayed where they were and didn't have to be made again.

What troubles me is that the 171,000 tonnes ZIN kept standing never count toward the rate, since they never became waste. Had the towers come down whole and been built again, both of the rate's counts would have grown: crushed rubble counts as recycled material, and new concrete and steel as material used. Suppose the new structure weighed about what was kept, 171,000 tonnes. Then crushing more than about one tonne in eight of the extra rubble into aggregate, some 22,000 tonnes, would have lifted the rate, because that share would be above the 11.4% the rate already stood at. Keeping the building adds to neither count, so the rate can't see the choice that kept an estimated 21,400 tonnes of CO2 out of the air.

By weighing it, IKEA's kitchens cut their food waste per meal by more than half

By weighing it, IKEA's kitchens cut their food waste per meal by more than half

In 2024, the EU threw away about 129 kg of food for every person, more than half of it in our homes. All the land, water and work that went into growing it cannot be recovered by putting it in the right bin.

Some kitchens are choosing to throw less of it away. In the restaurants of IKEA stores run by Ingka, which makes about 87% of IKEA's sales, kitchen teams began weighing their food waste on smart scales from Winnow and Leanpath. By the end of August 2022, the close of Ingka's 2022 financial year, they had cut the food they threw away per meal served by 57.8% from its 2017 level. That saved the equivalent of more than 20 million meals over four years, and 36,000 tonnes of CO2e. By August 2024, the cut per meal had reached 60.5%. Ingka has held a stake in Winnow since 2019.

The rate counts every tonne the same, whatever producing it costs the climate

The rate counts every tonne the same, whatever producing it costs the climate

We see what has been made. We seldom see what had to make way for it.

Not every tonne costs the climate the same, and the rate can't tell them apart, because it counts only weight. This is everything the EU used in 2022, in four kinds of material. Each ribbon runs from a material's share of the weight to its share of the climate cost of producing it, burning fuels not counted.

Sand, gravel, stone and other minerals are more than half of the EU's material use by weight, 52.3%, but only 11.4% of the climate cost of producing materials, so the tonnes that dominate the rate do the least harm to the climate per tonne. Europe also depends on others for very few of them: only about 3% come from outside the EU. By the rate's count, ZIN's concrete is this kind of tonne, though the cement in it costs the climate far more than gravel does.

Costing the climate little does not mean costing the earth little. Sand in a riverbed helps hold its banks in place. In Vietnam's Mekong Delta, taking too much of it is helping to bring down riverbanks that people have built their homes on.

Biomass, the crops, feed and wood we grow and harvest, is 22.7% of the weight but 45.7% of the climate cost, the largest share of the four. On average, tonne for tonne, producing it costs the climate about nine times what minerals do, and metal ore about 14 times. The food IKEA's kitchens stopped throwing away is biomass, yet the rate counts a tonne of it the same as a tonne of ZIN's concrete.

Most of the damage we do to the life on land comes from what we grow and harvest from it. Some of the land our coffee and chocolate grow on was forest not long ago.

Closer to home, one in three of Europe's bee, butterfly and hoverfly species is declining. Among them is Ireland's On the Mullet Peninsula in north-west Mayo, BirdWatch Ireland keeps its reserves as flower-rich meadow for it, Belmullet plants for it, and the All-Ireland Pollinator Plan names it among the rare species to save. [Photo credit], once found across the island and now holding on in a few places on the west coast.

Metals cost us land too. In Sulawesi, forest cover fell nearly twice as much around nickel mines as in villages without them. Nickel goes into stainless steel and into some of the batteries we are counting on to leave fossil fuels behind. Changing what we use doesn't free us from asking how much we use.

A better target would count what materials cost, not what they weigh

What would help, first, is saying plainly what the target asks. Recycling could close the ring alone only by reaching almost all of our waste, and only if each recycled tonne truly takes the place of new material, while using less alone would take a cut far deeper than any the EU has made. We will need both levers, and short of recycling almost all our waste, every route to 24% runs through using much less new material: about a third less even with 70% of our waste recycled, whatever each recycled tonne replaces.

Each year, each of us in the EU buys about a suitcase of clothes, shoes and household textiles. Making them takes about half a tonne of raw materials. We think about the space our clothes take up in our wardrobes, and seldom about the space they take up in the world.

That half tonne is part of our material footprint, the raw material our consumption needs, wherever it is dug or grown. The rate was given its number, 24%, in 2025, but neither the material footprint nor the consumption footprint, the harm our consumption does to the climate and to nature, has one, though the EU has said for years that both should fall. The coming Circular Economy Act could give them one, as Austria has already done for the material it uses.

It would also help to count what materials cost as well as what they weigh. Parts of EU law already count the carbon in materials, but the targets for circularity count tonnes, as do the EU's recycling targets for waste. Met by recycling 70% of waste and cutting the heaviest materials first, 24% would take 31% of the EU's new material away, yet cut, by a rough estimate, only about 8% of the climate cost of producing materials, burning fuels not counted, and about 5% of what the EU imports. Setting a number for the consumption footprint beside the rate would show what those tonnes cost.

Some of us are already keeping what we have: ZIN's owner kept its towers standing, and IKEA's kitchens learned to throw less food away.

Most of all, it would help to make what we have last and to design what we make next to need less material: using things for longer, wasting less of what we grow, and designing products and buildings to last, to be repaired and to use less from the start. Why should the life of a phone be set by the life of its battery? For homes, the International Resource Panel found that using them more intensively could cut the emissions of their materials by up to 70% by 2050 in the G7, against 14 to 18% from better recycling and 8 to 10% from designs that use less material.

I began with Ireland's number, so I'll end there. In February 2026, Ireland set its own goal, a rate of 12% by 2030, half the EU's. Held to the EU's 24%, as every country is in this story, it is much further away. Even if Ireland recycled every tonne of its waste, the same model takes its rate only to about 11%, less than halfway, whatever each recycled tonne replaces, because most of the waste it treats is soil and rock, and most of that goes back into the ground as fill.

Recycling 70% of its waste, Ireland would still need almost three quarters less new material to reach 24%, and more than a third less even for its own 12%.

Ireland is short of homes, and asking it to use so much less new material can sound like asking it to build fewer of them. Yet thousands of homes were empty on the night of each of the last three censuses. Some of what Ireland needs is already standing, though empty homes can't meet the whole need: many need major repair or are in the wrong places. Some of us still need more of what materials give us, which is all the more reason not to waste what we have. The Grand Parc estate's G, H and I blocks, before and after: Lacaton & Vassal, Frédéric Druot and Christophe Hutin, for Aquitanis. [Photo credit], 530 homes were given more light, more space and winter gardens without being torn down, while the people living in them stayed.

I rebuilt the EU's rate from Eurostat's own tables to find that out, so that I could move each lever myself, and every figure here comes from that model or from a source I checked; the notes at the end say which.

The drawings in this story are terrazzo, and just below is the floor itself, made from what was left over and set to last. That, too, is progress, even if the rate cannot see it.

About the drawings

The disc in the ring, the heaps of food and the flecks behind every chart are drawn as terrazzo: chips of stone set in a binder, then ground flat and polished. Builders have long made floors this way from what was left over. Romans beat broken pottery, tile and brick into lime for floors and cisterns. In Venice, as the trade tells it, masons set the marble chips left from their paying work into the terraces of their own homes, which gave terrazzo, Italian for terrace, its name. Craftsmen from Friuli carried it to the United States from the 1870s, and divider strips and electric grinders made it common by the 1920s. Here the disc is new material, drawn as the floor it could one day become if we keep what we already have.

Method

Each recycled tonne here replaces half a new one. That is an assumption, and this is what changes if it replaces none or a whole one.

When recycling rises, I assume each extra recycled tonne replaces half a new tonne of the same material. This is an assumed midpoint, not an estimate. How much new material a recycled tonne displaces is set by markets, and it can be close to none (Zink, Geyer and Startz 2018). The two ends are recycled tonnes simply adding to new ones, as in the European Environment Agency's 2030 scenarios (ETC CE Report 2023/6), and each replacing a whole new tonne, which ETC CE Report 2025/3 calls an upper limit. How much new material has to go to reach 24% at a given level of recycling is the same under all three, so the 59.3% and 31.0% hold either way. What changes is how far recycling alone gets, and how much of a cut recycling stands in for.

The chart shows why. The blue line is the new material that has to go for 24% at each share of the EU's waste recycled, with waste held at 2022's tonnes, and it is the same whatever a recycled tonne replaces. The gold lines are the part of it that recycling stands in for, at half a tonne each and at a whole one; replacing none, recycling stands in for nothing, and all of the cut is new material not replaced. Where a gold line meets the blue one, recycling alone closes the ring: at about 93% of waste recycled at half a tonne, and about 87% at a whole one. The cut falls toward nothing only as recycling nears every tonne of waste, the soil, rubble, mine waste and ash included. At 70% recycled, 31.0% has to go either way.

Replacement could also fall below none: a recycled tonne can end up adding to new production (Zink, Geyer and Startz 2018). For scale only, one model of the copper market finds that a lasting rise in scrap displaces about half a tonne of mined copper per tonne (Ryter et al. 2022). That is one metal, and not the basis for my midpoint.

When the EU uses less new material, I hold its waste and recycling at 2022's tonnes. The European Environment Agency's own scenarios for the doubling do the same, cutting material use while keeping recycled material constant (ETC CE Report 2023/6). The record supports it over a few years. In the five two-year steps since 2004 when the EU used less material, recycled material held or rose in three. From 2008 to 2010, material use fell 14.0% while recycled material rose 3.0%. In the other two it fell, by 0.6% from 2012 to 2014 and by 7.6% from 2018 to 2020 (my calculation from Eurostat). Most of the waste that comes back, rubble above all, comes from buildings and goods put in place years or decades earlier, so it doesn't fall with this year's use (ETC CE Report 2023/6).

Over longer spans, what comes back follows what goes in more closely. Growing stocks are what keep the loop open, and stocks that stopped growing would make closed loops more possible (Krausmann et al. 2017). In the United States, stocks held near today's level would give a steady flow of waste back after 2050 (Streeck et al. 2021). Once stocks stop growing, a lasting cut in use would in time bring less waste back too. Neither paper models that, so it is my reading. Some cuts shorten the arc sooner: burning less fuel and biomass for energy, or wasting less food, leaves less to recycle (ETC CE Report 2023/6).

This has limits: the largest falls on record are 14.0% in two years, from 2008 to 2010, and 6.2%, from 2022 to 2024 (estimated), far from 59.3%. A cut of 59.3% with all of 2022's waste still coming back would have to come wholly from material that doesn't come back as waste, such as fuel burned, food eaten and what is added to buildings and infrastructure, and it would take 85.7% of it. So 59.3% is a floor: if recycling slipped as use fell, the cut needed would go deeper. The model also assumes nothing else replaces the material that is cut, and it treats all minerals alike.

Using less lifts the rate because the rate is recycled material over all material used, recycled material included. In 2022 the EU used 0.13 t of recycled material for every tonne of new material. A tonne not used lifts the rate only if it takes less than that with it. With recycling held, it takes none. If recycling followed use down, a tonne of fossil fuels would take about 0.03 t with it and a tonne of biomass about 0.10 t, so cutting them would still lift the rate, while a tonne of minerals would take about 0.15 t and a tonne of metal ores about 0.25 t, so cutting them would lower it (env_wassd).

Doubling the rate has three figures. The European Environment Agency doubles 2020's rate: 11.7% to 23.4% in 2023 (EEA 2023), and 22.4% on its indicator page, from 2020's rate as since revised to 11.2%. The Clean Industrial Deal aims for 24%, "from 11.8% today", which the Commission calls doubling a rate of about 12% and the EEA a slight increase on the doubling. 24% is the figure used here.

By new material I mean what Eurostat calls domestic material consumption: the material extracted in the EU, plus imports, less exports, by weight (env_ac_mfa). Imports and exports count at the weight of the goods themselves, not of all the raw material used to make them abroad, so it isn't a footprint. Recycled material comes on top of it: the rate is recycled material over the two together.

Eurostat counts as recycled only waste recycled into materials (operations R2 to R11), not waste burned for energy or used as backfill, from its waste treatment statistics (env_wastrt), net of waste traded across borders for recycling (env_ac_cur metadata, sections 3.1, 3.4 and 18.1). Rebuilt from those tonnes, before trade, 2022's rate comes to 11.2%, against 11.4% published, possibly because the two downloads come from different releases; with backfilling counted, it would be 14.6%. To split by material I use env_wassd, which counts less waste (1,675 Mt treated and 741 Mt recycled, against env_wastrt's 1,971 Mt and 809 Mt), so its material shares are scaled to the rate's totals. Other products and waste for final treatment, 0.5% of EU material use in 2022, are left out of the shards.

From 2024's rate, 24% by 2030 takes a rise of about 2% of material used a year, against a rise from 10.7% in 2010 to 12.2% in 2024 (my calculation from Eurostat). The European Environment Agency's warning is in its 2023 briefing: "Enhanced waste recycling alone will not be sufficient for the EU to achieve the target" (EEA 2023).

Neither that briefing nor the report behind it works out how far either lever alone would have to go: both test set levels of each lever and combine them (EEA 2023; ETC CE Report 2023/6, sections 4.3.1 and 4.4). In the briefing's combined scenario, recycling 70% of treated waste, using 15% less material than in 2020 and a third less fossil fuel lift the rate to about 22% by 2030, short of the 23.4% it aims for. Recycling 90% of all waste would bring the EU close to the target only by 2050 (EEA 2023).

The European Topic Centre on Circular Economy and Resource Use, which works for the agency, went further in 2025. To double the rate from 2020's 11.6% to the 23.2% it takes as the target, it cut fossil fuels by 34% and raised recycling to what it calls a theoretical maximum of about 69% of all waste, with each recycled tonne replacing a whole new one, which it says is likely an overestimation. It then worked out the cut still needed: 26% less material in all than in 2020 (ETC CE Report 2025/3, key messages and sections 3.1 and 4.1 to 4.4). That is one route, from another base year and for another target, and it sits close to the 31.0% less here with 70% of waste recycled. It doesn't work out how far either lever alone would have to go, or what any country would need.

The waste that comes back is the waste the EU treats, which weighed as much as 30.9% of its material consumption in 2022 (env_wastrt, env_ac_mfa). 68.2% of it is soil and dredged spoil, rubble from construction and demolition, and other mineral waste, most of which comes from mines and quarries (env_wastrt, env_wasgen).

Ireland's rate was the second lowest of the EU's 27 in 2022 and 2023, after Romania's, and joint second lowest with Finland's in Eurostat's estimate for 2024 (env_ac_cur). Most of its treated waste is soil and rock, and most of the waste it doesn't recycle is backfilled (env_wastrt). Most of the EU's waste not recycled in 2022 was landfilled or backfilled too; the rest went to other kinds of disposal or was burned. Backfilling, using waste to fill land that has been dug out, doesn't count as recycling in the rate (env_ac_cur metadata).

Recycling every tonne has never been enough to go far past a quarter. In every reporting year from 2010 to 2022, recycling all of the EU's treated waste would have lifted the rate to between 25.5% and 28.1%, with each extra recycled tonne replacing half a new one. If recycled tonnes replaced none, it would have been 23.7% to 25.8%; if each replaced a whole new tonne, 27.5% to 30.9% (my calculation from Eurostat). The 59.3% less new material that closes the ring by using less alone is also my arithmetic, not a published figure; Method sets out what it assumes, and why cutting minerals or metal ores would lower the rate if recycling fell with use.

The 19 of 27 countries that would still have to use less come from each country's own ring, set out in the note on every EU country's ring.

When recycling rises in the ring, the new tonnes it replaces are split across materials by the waste not yet recycled: 86.5% minerals, 9.5% biomass, 3.2% fossil fuels and 0.9% metal ores (env_wassd). Sorted by Eurostat's waste categories instead, 75.8% of it is major mineral waste, about 82% with ash and mineral residues (env_wastrt). Those tonnes leave a disc whose shards cover each group's share of the EU's material consumption: 52.0% minerals, 23.3% biomass, 19.3% fossil fuels and 5.5% metal ores (env_ac_mfa).

The practical limits in the reveal's third card come from studies of particular materials, not from an EU total. At EU plants handling plastic packaging, sorting keeps a median of 37% to 85% of what comes in, depending on the plastic, and reprocessing 66% to 88% (Antonopoulos, Faraca and Tonini 2021, JRC); that evidence covers plastic packaging only. Europe's paper industry says its loop has to be topped up with fresh fiber (Cepi, 2022). Copper in steel scrap can't be removed commercially once melted, so only new iron or cleaner scrap brings it down (Daehn, Cabrera Serrenho and Allwood 2017), and in the EU flat steel, the sheet that car bodies are made from, comes almost entirely from primary production (UBA, AutoRess Factsheet 10, 2025). Concrete's European standard, EN 206, recommends limits on recycled aggregate by use, and national rules set the binding ones. Those limits aren't what keeps rubble out of concrete: the aggregate producers the JRC surveyed sold between 0 and 3% of their recycled aggregate for concrete, the rest going mostly to backfilling and roads (JRC 2023).

The climate cost removed is each group's share of its own consumption removed, times its share of the climate impact of making the EU's materials (ETC CE Report 2025/3, Figure 3-4). For the tonnes recycling replaces, that uses each group's average impact per tonne, which flatters recycling: recycled aggregate stands in for natural sand, gravel and crushed stone, not for cement, and concrete made with it often needs more cement (JRC 2023). Nor does it count the energy recycling itself uses. So the 3.6% of climate cost removed when recycling alone closes the ring is generous. Every climate figure here is a rough estimate, not a modeled saving in emissions: each assumes a material's climate cost falls in proportion to its use, at its average per tonne, and applies shares from the ETC's footprint models, which follow materials back through their supply chains, to Eurostat's tonnes of material consumed.

Imports are each group's imports from outside the EU in 2022 (env_ac_mfa). They were 70.9% of the fossil fuels and 50.3% of the metal ores entering the EU's economy, against 12.2% of its biomass and 3.2% of its minerals. Any route's cut in imports assumes each group's imports fall in proportion to its use.

Following Eurostat's method, waste the EU exports for recycling counts as circular and waste it imports for recycling is deducted. In 2022, across the four groups, it exported 36.4 Mt and imported 40.7 million (env_ac_sd), so trade lowered its circular use slightly. Metals ran the other way: 19.5 Mt went out against 5.6 million in, and exports were 19.5% of metals' circular use. Because env_wassd's tonnes are scaled to the rate's totals (Method), these splits by material are approximate.

Recycling lifted the rate slowly; its biggest jump came from using less, in a crisis

The line joins the published rate every second year, the years for which countries report their waste; Eurostat estimates the waste of the years between (env_ac_cur metadata). Each step of the waterfall ends where the line does. The shaded bands mark the two-year step in which each crisis cut the EU's material use, by Eurostat's yearly material accounts (env_ac_mfa): 2009 in the financial crisis, 2020 in COVID and 2023 in the energy crisis. They name the shocks, not causes: the bars show what each lever did. Eurostat names the same three shocks in the EU's imports, the energy crisis for the shrinking volume of fossil fuels it brought in (Eurostat, "Physical imports and exports," data from June 2026).

Each two-year change in the rate is split into what recycling did and what using less or more did (my calculation). The rate is circular use over circular use plus material consumption, so circular use is worked back from the published rate (env_ac_cur) and consumption (env_ac_mfa). Recycling's part of a step is the change that circular use alone would have made, averaged over holding consumption at its starting and at its ending level; using less or more is the rest, so the two add up to the change.

The rate is published to one decimal, and circular use worked back from it carries that rounding. So a step's recycling part can be off by as much as 0.1% of material used either way, which weighs most on the smallest bars and in the country explorer. I keep the published rate rather than a rebuild from tonnes, which gives a slightly lower 2022 rate (Method).

The work lost in the financial crisis: from 2008 to 2010, the number of people aged 15 to 64 in work in the EU fell from 189.9 million to 183.7 million, and in construction from 15.8 million to 14.1 million (Eurostat, lfsa_egan2; 2008 is the first year of its breakdown by industry and is marked as a break in series). Both fell with the recession, not because the EU used less material: the card names the crisis as their shared cause.

The last step, 2022 to 2024, rests on Eurostat's estimates of the 2024 rate and material use, and is marked as an estimate. For 2025 there is no rate yet, only Eurostat's imputed material use. By it, the EU used 3.4% more material in 2025, about half (51.9%) of what it had cut from 2022 to 2024. Minerals made up 64.2% of the rise and recovered 77.6% of their fall, while fossil fuels fell a further 1.8%.

That the fall in fossil fuels followed years of decisions is the European Environment Agency's reading, on a different measure, the material footprint: fossil fuels are the only group whose footprint fell from 2010 to 2024, by 36%, which it says could be put down to the EU's climate policies and to the recent high energy prices (EEA, "Europe's material footprint," November 2025).

Bord na Móna's end to peat harvesting followed the courts as well as a choice. A High Court judgment of September 2019 found that extracting peat from bogs over 30 hectares needed planning permission, so Bord na Móna, after its last full harvest in 2018 and a partial one in 2019, suspended harvesting in 2020, and on January 15, 2021 made the suspension permanent and withdrew its planning applications (RTÉ; Bord na Móna). Under the Peatlands Climate Action Scheme it is rewetting about 33,000 hectares of its former production bogs, with €108 million from the EU's recovery funds, and it moved the staff who had harvested peat, about 350 people, into that work (EPA, State of the Environment Report 2024; Agriland). Peat is counted as a fossil fuel in the material accounts. Ireland's peat extraction fell from about 4.5 Mt in 2018 to about 0.4 Mt a year from 2021 to 2024, so some goes on outside Bord na Móna (CSO, Material Flow Accounts 2024; env_ac_mfa). Ireland also began importing horticultural peat, 3,600 tonnes from Latvia in September 2021 (Irish Times), though that year it exported about 12 times as much peat as it imported (env_ac_mfa; my calculation).

Your turn: close the EU's ring

Sources: Eurostat; climate shares and total, ETC CE Report 2025/3; recycling scenarios, EEA; burning fuels, IEA. EU, 2022. Removals: my calculation.

The game runs on the same model as the story. Waste is held at 2022's 1,971 Mt treated. The knob starts at the 41.1% of it recycled in 2022 by the rate's own count (Eurostat's waste statistics give 40.8%), and each extra recycled tonne replaces half a new one, an assumed midpoint (Method), split across materials by the waste not yet recycled, as in the ring's note. A tap takes a quarter of one material's 2022 use, so the ring closes in 6 to 13 taps at 2022's recycling, whatever the route: fewest by cutting minerals first, the heaviest, and most by cutting the other three first. Recycling replaces first, and taps cut only what it hasn't, so a cut gives way as recycling rises. Its endings match the story: 59.3% less new material at 2022's recycling, 31.0% at 70%, and recycling alone closing the ring between 93% and 94% of waste recycled.

Climate cost removed is each material's share removed of its own consumption, times its share of the climate impact of making the EU's materials (ETC CE Report 2025/3, Figure 3-4), applied to Eurostat's tonnes. As in the ring's note, the credit for recycling's replaced tonnes uses average impact per tonne, which flatters recycling. The shares are of 2,890 Mt of CO2e, the climate impact of the raw materials used in the EU in 2022 (ETC CE Report 2025/3, Table 3-1). That is a footprint of the EU's final demand, measured to the factory gate, so it can't be set beside the EU's own emissions. Built from averages per tonne, the share is a rough guide to the climate cost of the material removed, not an estimate of the emissions a cut would save. Burning fuels isn't counted, and for oil and gas that is most of their climate cost: worldwide, producing, moving and refining them caused just under 15% of energy-related greenhouse gas emissions, and using them another 40% (IEA, Emissions from Oil and Gas Operations in Net Zero Transitions, 2023). The report doesn't say Figure 3-4's shares come from the same calculation as the total, though both rest on the same models (FIGARO, 2024 edition, and EXIOBASE 3.8.2).

Imports removed are measured against the 1,660 Mt the EU imported from outside it in 2022 (env_ac_mfa), and assume each material's imports fall in proportion to its use. A material with nothing left counts as all removed.

Keeping a building standing cut its upfront carbon by a third; tearing it down could have scored better

Sources: Leroy, Gomez and Nosiglia (Bureau Greisch), IABSE 2025; Befimmo; Eurostat; Circle Economy; Steingrimsson, Davidsdottir, Petursson and Reck, Resources, Conservation and Recycling; Christis and Vercalsteren 2021; ETC CE Report 2023/6; heat deaths, Barnes and others, Nature Health 2026. How the rate counts tearing down against keeping, and applying it to ZIN: my reading.

The study is the structural engineers' own, Bureau Greisch's paper on ZIN for the IABSE Congress in Ghent, 2025; the building's owner is Befimmo. Of the towers' original structural mass, about 85%, 171,000 tonnes, was kept: the cores and the basements (section 3.1). The 85% is the engineers' share of structural mass. Befimmo puts what it retained at more than 60% of the existing structure, without saying how it measures it (press release, October 13, 2025). The floor slabs came down, about 30,000 tonnes by my arithmetic (the other 15% of the roughly 201,000 tonnes the 85% implies), which matches the 30,000 tonnes of rubble the abstract says was recycled into certified aggregate.

That is the only tonnage the paper's text gives for the rubble. Section 3.3 says the aggregate from the demolished floors was sorted, crushed and mixed into concrete for the new building, in a loop within 15 km of the site. Its Figure 5 shows 3,500 tonnes of the highest grade, A+, going into ZIN's new concrete, and doesn't say what became of the rest. The card's almost six tonnes kept standing for every tonne recycled uses the abstract's 30,000 tonnes; counting only the 3,500 that went back into ZIN, it would be about 49.

The carbon is upfront carbon only, for the construction stage (module A), in kilograms of CO2, not CO2e; the paper names no method or database (section 5). It is measured against "a realistic benchmark for efficient new construction," which the paper doesn't describe further. Against it, the structure's upfront carbon fell by about half, 184 kg per square meter or 21,400 tonnes in all, and the whole building's by 33% (section 5). By the paper's Figure 14, the whole saving is in the foundations and the load-bearing structure; finishes, facade and building services are level. It is saved once, when the building goes up.

The paper gives ZIN's 377 kg per square meter without saying what it covers. The chart's new build, at 561, adds the 184 kg saved, reading the 377 as the whole building, since 184 of 561 is the paper's 33%.

Rubble crushed into aggregate counts as recycling (operation R5), and so as circular use, while rubble used to fill in excavations doesn't (env_ac_cur metadata). Tearing a building down and building again adds both recycled rubble and new material to what is used. That lifts the rate whenever the recycled rubble is a larger share of the extra material than the rate already is, 11.4% for the EU in 2022, which holds for a building that is mostly concrete, since the EU recycles most of its construction and demolition rubble. Keeping the building adds to neither count. One building barely moves the EU's rate; what it shows is which way the rate moves. The 30,000 tonnes of rubble from ZIN's demolished floors count as recycling under the same rule.

With extra recycled rubble e and extra new material d, the rate rises when e/(e+d) is above the current rate, 11.4%, so when e is more than 0.129 times d. With d at 171,000 tonnes, about what ZIN kept, that is about 22,000 tonnes. A heavier new structure raises the bar in proportion, and so does measuring against a country rate higher than the EU's.

That this makes ZIN score worse than tearing its towers down and building again is my reading of Eurostat's method. The principle is in print, though not for buildings under Eurostat's rate. In its 2023 report on Denmark, Circle Economy wrote that demolishing all of Denmark's buildings and recycling them would sharply raise its own circularity measure, which divides recycled material by a footprint rather than by the material a country uses, and called it "an undesirable means for increasing circularity" (pp. 30 to 31). A study of Iceland's rate by Steingrimsson and others found that Eurostat's rate can fall when environmental performance improves, as when food waste is prevented, since preventing waste also removes what would have been recycled. Other published work says only that the rate leaves out strategies like making things last (Christis and Vercalsteren 2021), and reads longer lifetimes as lifting it by cutting new material (ETC CE Report 2023/6), which holds against building new but not against tearing down and building again. I found no one who has worked out when demolition lifts the rate, or tested it on a real building.

The heat deaths are from Barnes and others, "Heat-related urban mortality attributable to human-induced climate change in Europe in summer 2026" (Nature Health, 2026): 50,391 heat-related deaths from June to August 2026 in 854 cities of the EU, the UK, Norway and Switzerland, home to about 40% of their people, of which 32,619, about 65%, are attributable to human-caused warming. They are modeled from each city's temperatures and its link between heat and deaths, not counted from death records, and leave out a deadly heat wave in May. The card links the carbon kept out of the air to that warming in general; it puts no share of the deaths on any building.

The rate counts every tonne the same, whatever producing it costs the climate

Sources: ETC CE Report 2025/3; Eurostat; International Resource Panel; Hackney and others 2020; UNEP; Witbaard and others 2025; WWF; WRI; European Commission; All-Ireland Pollinator Plan; BirdWatch Ireland; Lo and others 2024. EU, 2022, for the ribbon. Per tonne: my calculation.

Both sides of the ribbon come from ETC CE Report 2025/3, Figure 3-4: the EU-27's material use in 2022 by weight, and the climate impact of producing those materials, from extraction to the factory gate. The weight shares are the ones the report pairs with its climate shares, so the ribbon keeps them. They differ from the Eurostat shares the ring's disc uses (the ring's note) by at most 0.6% of material use: biomass is 22.7% here, against 23.3%.

Because the climate shares stop at the factory gate, they count what it takes to extract and refine fossil fuels, not what burning them releases, so fossil fuels look mild at 26.6%. Biomass has the largest share of the impact, but not the most per tonne: metal ores carry 16.3% of the impact on 5.5% of the weight, about three times their share, against biomass's two. The biomass card's per-tonne figures divide each material's share of the climate impact by its share of the weight, against minerals': a tonne of fossil fuels comes to 6.3 times a tonne of minerals, biomass to 9.2 times and metal ores to 13.6 times. ZIN's kept structure includes its reinforcing steel, which the paper doesn't split out, so its tag sits on minerals. The minerals card's "about 3%" is the share of the minerals entering the EU's economy that came from outside it (env_ac_mfa; the ring's note gives each group's).

The ribbon measures only the climate cost of producing materials. The harms the cards name beside it, to rivers, forests and the life on land, come from other sources and other measures, and none of the places named is where the EU's tonnes in this story come from: only about 3% of its minerals came from outside the EU.

Sand: in the lower Mekong, about 50 Mt of sand is taken from the river each year, against about 6 Mt that flows into the delta, enough to lower the riverbed and make riverbanks unstable, putting homes and roads at risk (Hackney and others 2020, Nature Sustainability); dams and sinking land add to it. Worldwide, about 50 billion tonnes of sand and gravel are used each year, faster than nature replaces them (UNEP, Sand and Sustainability, 2026). Most of the EU's sand and gravel is dug within it, and taking it has costs here too: off the Dutch coast, the seabed's life in pits dug for sand still differed from the seabed around them 12 years later (Witbaard and others 2025).

Biomass: growing and harvesting it causes more than 90% of the world's biodiversity loss and water stress related to land use (International Resource Panel, Global Resources Outlook 2024). The same report puts all extraction and processing of materials at more than 55% of the world's greenhouse gas emissions, but that counts fossil fuels to the point of use, so it isn't the ribbon's measure. In 2017, the EU's imports were linked to 16% of the tropical deforestation tied to international trade, second only to China's; soy, palm oil and beef led, then wood, cocoa and coffee (WWF 2021, on data from Pendrill and others and Trase, the UK still counted in the EU). The EU has been the second-largest importer of deforestation since 2014, and six commodities, beef, palm oil, soy, cocoa, coffee and rubber, made up 85% of the forest loss linked to its imports (WRI, October 2026, on Trase data).

Pollinators: the Commission's figure, from the European Red Lists, is that around one in three bee, butterfly and hoverfly species in Europe is declining, and one in ten bee and butterfly species is threatened with extinction (COM(2023) 35). The Great Yellow Bumblebee is endangered in Ireland and vulnerable across Europe. It was found across the island before 1960 and is now restricted to the Mullet Peninsula in north-west Mayo and nearby, with small populations on the mainland and on Achill (All-Ireland Pollinator Plan). The All-Ireland Pollinator Plan for 2021 to 2025 focuses on rare species like it, and BirdWatch Ireland's reserves on the Mullet hold its largest numbers.

Nickel: across 7,721 villages in Sulawesi from 2011 to 2018, forest cover fell by 4.4% of village land where nickel was mined, against 2.4% in similar villages without mining, which the authors call a near doubling. For the people living there the outcomes were mixed: early in mining, living standards, education and the local environment worsened while health, infrastructure and social ties improved, and later the environment kept getting worse, most in the poorest villages (Lo and others 2024, One Earth).

A better target would count what materials cost, not what they weigh

Sources: Eurostat; ETC CE Reports 2023/6 and 2025/3; European Commission; Decision (EU) 2022/591; European Parliament; Council of the EU; European Environment Agency; Austria's Circular Economy Strategy; Regulation (EU) 2023/956; Regulation (EU) 2024/3110; Directive (EU) 2024/1275; Directive 2008/98/EC; Haupt and Hellweg 2019; EPA Research Report 458; Ireland's Circular Economy Strategy 2026 to 2028; CSO Census 2022; EEA; SWD(2023) 59; Lacaton & Vassal and the European Commission; International Resource Panel; Krausmann et al. 2017; Haas et al. 2015. Climate impact and imports removed, and the rate past 2030: my calculation.

Recycling closing the ring alone only by reaching almost all our waste is the 93.4% of waste recycled in the Method table, where the cut it brings is 8.1%. The cut far deeper than any the EU has made is the 59.3% needed with recycling held at 2022, against the largest two-year fall on record, 14.0% (Method).

The 2020 Circular Economy Action Plan set the doubling in the same sentence as a pledge to reduce the EU's consumption footprint (COM(2020) 98, Introduction). The Clean Industrial Deal of 2025 made 24% by 2030 one of its targets, citing that plan, and gave as its reasons making the most of limited resources, depending less on others, resilience, lower production costs and less CO2 (COM(2025) 85, sections 1 and 3). It never mentions using less material: "footprint" appears once, for labels showing consumers a product's carbon footprint, and "consumption" only for energy. The Commission plans to adopt the Circular Economy Act in the fourth quarter of 2026.

The 8th Environment Action Programme lists, among the conditions its objectives "shall require" of the Commission, member states and others "as appropriate," "significantly decreasing the Union's material and consumption footprints to bring them into planetary boundaries as soon as possible, including through the introduction of Union 2030 reduction targets, as appropriate" (Decision (EU) 2022/591, Article 3(s)). The material footprint is the raw material, in tonnes, needed to make what the EU consumes; the consumption footprint is the environmental impact of that consumption, scored by the Joint Research Centre in 16 impact categories, climate change among them. The Commission's monitoring targets for both are only to "significantly decrease" them, while most of the targets beside them carry a number, the rate's doubling among them (COM(2022) 357). The European Parliament asked for binding 2030 targets for both footprints (resolution of February 10, 2021), and the Council invited the Commission to assess "ambitious and economically feasible science-based targets" for them (conclusions of June 17, 2024, paragraph 28(c)). The European Environment Agency says circular policies should extend beyond waste "to possibly include targets on resource use or material footprint" (EEA 2024, executive summary). Austria's strategy pairs an 18% rate target for 2030 with bringing material use down to 14 tonnes per person by 2030.

The suitcase and the half tonne are the European Environment Agency's figures for 2022: each person in the EU consumed about 19 kg of clothing, footwear and household textiles, "enough to fill a large suitcase," and making what EU households bought took about 523 kg of raw materials and 323 square meters of land per person. Those are material footprints, counted back through supply chains around the world, not the material consumed that the rate uses (EEA, textiles module of its Circularity Metrics Lab, and "Circularity of the EU textiles value chain in numbers," 2025).

At 70% of waste recycled, the EU still needs 31.0% less new material: recycling replaces 4.5%, mostly minerals, and nothing replaces the other 26.5% (Method). If that 26.5% is taken whole material by whole material, heaviest first, that is minerals alone, and the cut removes 7.8% of the climate impact of making the EU's materials and 4.6% of what it imports from outside the EU. Taken by the climate cost of producing each tonne instead, burning fuels not counted, metal ores first and then biomass, the same tonnes remove 59.5% of that climate cost and 26.4% of imports. Counting burning would put fossil fuels higher in that order. Both are counted as in the game's note, with the same credit to recycling, and are rough estimates, as the ring's note explains.

Parts of EU law already count the carbon in materials. Since January 1, 2026, importers of cement, iron and steel, aluminum, fertilizers, electricity and hydrogen owe certificates for the greenhouse gases embedded in what they import, priced on the EU Emissions Trading System (Regulation (EU) 2023/956). Construction products' declarations of performance must cover their climate change effects over their life cycle from January 8, 2026, but only once a product family's harmonized standard has been renewed under the new regulation, and the old standards may stay in use until 2040 (Regulation (EU) 2024/3110, Article 15(3) and Annex II). From January 1, 2028, new buildings over 1,000 square meters must have their carbon over their whole life calculated and disclosed in their energy performance certificate, and all new buildings from 2030 (Directive (EU) 2024/1275). The EU's waste targets, by contrast, are set by weight, such as at least 70% by weight of non-hazardous construction and demolition waste reused, recycled or otherwise recovered, backfilling included (Directive 2008/98/EC, Article 11(2)). That a measure by weight misses what materials cost is published: mass-based indicators such as recycling rates "fail to cover the environmental perspective" (Haupt and Hellweg 2019), the rate "does not directly consider other environmental pressures such as greenhouse gas emissions" (EPA Research Report 458), and a higher rate "does not necessarily lead to lower environmental impacts" (ETC CE Report 2025/3).

The remedy of getting more from what is already built, by using it more intensively, making it last longer and designing it better, is Krausmann et al. 2017's. The figures for homes are the International Resource Panel's, for the G7 in 2050, and count the emissions of the materials homes need: up to 70% less from using homes more intensively, 14 to 18% from better recycling of construction materials and 8 to 10% from designs that use less material (IRP 2020, factsheet). The EEA likewise calls for more intensity of use per product and much longer product lifetimes, and for putting the reduction of resource use first (EEA 2024, executive summary).

The Commission estimates that throwing away consumer goods that could still be repaired causes 261 Mt of CO2e, uses 30 Mt of resources and makes 35 Mt of waste in the EU each year (SWD(2023) 59, the impact assessment for what became the Repair Directive, as cited by the EEA).

Ireland's goal of 12% by 2030 is in its Whole of Government Circular Economy Strategy 2026 to 2028, published in February 2026 (Department of Climate, Energy and the Environment); its first strategy aimed for a rate above the EU average by 2030. Recycling every tonne of the waste Ireland treated in 2022 would take its rate to 10.5%, 10.9% or 11.5%, as each recycled tonne replaces none, half or a whole new one (Method). Held to the EU's 24%, that is less than half at every replacement. With 70% recycled, it would still need 73.1% less new material to reach 24%, and 37.7% less for its own 12%, each the same under all three. 69.9% of the waste it treats is soil and rock, and 51.9% is backfilled (env_wastrt). My calculation. Census 2022 counted 163,433 vacant homes, holiday homes left out, 8% of Ireland's housing stock. The census counts vacancy on one night, and some of those homes may have been lived in again soon after. Just under 48,000 were vacant in both 2016 and 2022, and 23,072 in all three censuses of 2011, 2016 and 2022, which the CSO reads as a sign of longer vacancy, not proof of it (CSO, Census 2022 Profile 2, Housing in Ireland). Many empty homes need major work or are where few people want to live, so they can't meet the whole shortage (Irish Times, June 24, 2022). In Bordeaux's Grand Parc, Lacaton & Vassal, Frédéric Druot and Christophe Hutin transformed three occupied social housing blocks of 530 homes, finished in 2017, adding winter gardens and balconies and new glazing while the residents stayed; it improved homes that were there and added none (European Commission; the architects). The two rings beside these paragraphs are drawn as the country explorer draws Ireland's: its ring in 2022, and its ring with 70% of its waste recycled and the cut that closes it at 24%. Ireland's split of its waste by material needs care (the note on every EU country's ring), so which shards leave the disc is less certain than how much of it goes.

Past 2030, the rate will see even less. Once Europe stops adding to what it has built, a building kept standing is also rubble that never comes back to be recycled, so the rate will rise mostly as we burn less, not as we make things last. This is my arithmetic, not a published finding, and it assumes stocks stop growing. By the 0.13 t rule in Method, a tonne of fuel not burned takes only about 0.03 t of recycled material with it, while rubble that never comes back takes its whole recycled share. It rests in part on the ETC's finding that fossil fuels and biomass largely become emissions, while metals and minerals go into stock and become available for recycling later (ETC CE Report 2023/6, section 2.3.3; its section 2.3.2 adds that burning less leaves less to recycle, which the 0.03 t counts), and on Haas et al. 2015, which traces low circularity to energy use and to stocks that keep growing.

Each country's ring runs on the story's model (Method), with its own figures for 2022. Recycling rises to 70% of the waste the country treats, the European Environment Agency's scenario for the EU in 2030 (EEA 2023); Italy, which already recycles 85.6%, stays where it is. Each extra recycled tonne replaces half a new one, an assumed midpoint, and the tonnes it replaces leave the disc by the country's waste not yet recycled, as in the ring's note. The cut still needed then takes the same share of every material. For a country still short of 24% after recycling, how much new material has to go is the same whatever recycling replaces; only the split between what recycling stands in for and what isn't replaced changes. Tonnes per person are material consumption per person (env_ac_mfa). Each figure is rounded on its own, so what recycling replaces and what isn't replaced can add up to 0.1% more or less than its total cut.

At the midpoint, 19 of the 27 stay short of 24% with 70% of their waste recycled, as they do if each recycled tonne replaced a whole new one; if recycled tonnes replaced none, 23 would. The eight that pass are Austria, Bulgaria, Estonia, Finland, Luxembourg, Malta, the Netherlands and Sweden, Malta only just, at 24.1%. Bulgaria, Estonia, the Netherlands and Sweden would pass even if recycled tonnes replaced none (my calculation from Eurostat).

What sets the size of a country's cut is how much waste it treats for each tonne of material it consumes: from 5.7% in Latvia to 68.5% in the Netherlands (the EU's is in the ring's note). Treated waste is the waste treated in the country, so it can include waste brought in from abroad. Luxembourg treated 10.1 Mt in 2022 (env_wastrt), of which it had brought in 3.1 million to recycle (env_ac_sd), against 15.3 Mt of material consumed.

To split a country's waste by material, I scale env_wassd's tonnes to the rate's totals, as for the EU. For 15 countries that split needs care. Bulgaria, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania and Malta don't publish their trade in waste by material, and for the rest env_wassd's tonnes are more than half off the rate's: Austria, Belgium, Croatia, Cyprus, Ireland, Italy, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Latvia, the Netherlands, Spain and Sweden. In Ireland (biomass) and Cyprus (fossil fuels), one material comes out with negative circular use, because more of it was brought in to be recycled than was recycled. The split decides only which shards leave the disc. The card gives the split between what recycling replaces and what isn't replaced in total, which doesn't depend on it.

Four of the eight pass only by recycling their soil and rock, the soils, dredged spoil and other mineral waste, mostly from mining (env_wastrt: W126, W127 and W12B): Austria, Bulgaria, Finland and Luxembourg. If they recycled all the rest of their waste and their soil and rock only as in 2022, their rates would reach 16.6%, 9.4%, 7.9% and 14.2%. Estonia, Malta and the Netherlands pass without it, and Sweden's split is confidential. The European Environment Agency's 70% is of all treated waste, mineral waste included (ETC CE Report 2023/6, pp. 30 and 31).

Five countries carry a warning. Eurostat imputed Romania's material use for 2009 to 2019, and the minerals it reported then rose 42% from 2018 to 2020, from 314 to 445 Mt (env_ac_mfa). The waste Sweden recycled doubled from 2020 to 2022, from 17.2 to 33.9 Mt, while its recycling outside mineral wastes held at 7.2 to 7.8 million; the categories of its major mineral wastes are confidential for 2022 (env_wastrt). Estonia's, Finland's and Bulgaria's rates swing widely from one report to the next on recycling alone, much of it oil shale mining rock in Estonia, and soil and rock in Finland.

Germany's line rests on its National Circular Economy Strategy, adopted in December 2024, which pairs doubling its rate by 2030 with significantly reducing the primary raw material it uses by 2045 (BMUV, English summary). That aim is measured as raw material consumption, a footprint, not the material consumed in the ring, and the strategy gives no number for Germany.
