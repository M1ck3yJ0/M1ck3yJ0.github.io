Data analysis by Milcah M. Joseph · Eurostat data for 2022

The Leaky Loop

Europe wants to double how much of its material comes from recycled waste, to 24% by 2030. But most of what it uses never comes back as waste, so even recycling every tonne it treats would only get it to about the target. The lever with no ceiling is using less material.

1 · Where it goes

Of every 100 tonnes Europe uses, only 20 come back as waste

The rest is built into buildings and roads, burned or eaten, exported or spread on land. Recycling can only work with what comes back.

Source: Eurostat, env_ac_sd and env_wassd, EU, 2022.

How I worked this out

Eurostat's material flow accounts follow every tonne of material processed in the EU: extracted at home, imported, recycled or backfilled. I took the shares built into stock, burned or eaten (emissions), exported, spread on land (dissipative flows) and treated as waste, and rounded them to whole tonnes out of 100.

The soil and rock part of the 20 is approximate: soil and rock's share of all the waste Europe treats, 54%, applied to the waste in the flows.

2 · How far recycling can take itMy calculation

Even recycling every tonne it treats, Europe would only get to about its 24% target

0% less

With recycling as it is, reaching 24% would take 59% less material. Even recycling everything but soil and rock, it would take 42% less.

Source: my calculation on Eurostat env_ac_cur, env_ac_mfa and env_wastrt, 2022.

How I worked this out

The circularity rate is recycled waste divided by recycled waste plus material consumption. Starting from Eurostat's published rate, I added every tonne of waste each country treated but didn't recycle, holding consumption fixed. If each recycled tonne replaced a tonne of new material, the EU's limit would be 27.5%.

Soil and rock means soils, dredging spoils and other mineral waste, mostly from mining. The slider lowers consumption with recycling unchanged, which is static arithmetic: in reality, using less also means less waste later.

3 · Every countryMy calculation

Even recycling every tonne they treat, 15 of the 27 countries would stay below 24%

The limit follows how much waste a country treats for every tonne it uses, not how well it recycles. Sort by each to see.

Source: my calculation on Eurostat env_ac_cur, env_ac_mfa and env_wastrt, 2022. Tap a row for its figures.

How I worked this out

The same calculation as above, for each country. Across the 27, the limit tracks waste treated per tonne used almost exactly (correlation 0.98) and has nothing to do with the share recycled (correlation -0.26).

Mining waste lifts the limits of Finland, Bulgaria, Romania and Sweden. Sweden doesn't publish its soil and rock figures.

4 · Ten years onMy calculation

From 2012 to 2022, the rate rose 0.4 points while recycling's limit fell 1.8

At the last decade's pace, 0.04 points a year, the rate would be about 11.7% in 2030. Reaching 24% takes about 1.6 points a year, 40 times faster. Eurostat's 2024 figures, still partly estimates, show the other lever at work: Europe used 6% less material than in 2022, which alone added about 0.7 points. And what Europe builds now decides what comes back as waste decades from now.

Source: my calculation on Eurostat env_ac_cur, env_ac_mfa and env_wastrt, 2012 to 2024.

How I worked this out

Waste is reported every two years, so the chart uses even years. The 2024 values build on Eurostat's estimated rate for that year. Almost all of the limit's fall is soil and rock: without them, it stayed at about 15.5%.

To split the change, I recalculated 2012's rate twice: once with 2022's recycling (the recycling effect) and once with 2022's consumption (the consumption effect). The two add up to the total, give or take rounding.

5 · What it's made of

Half of what Europe uses is minerals, and a third is sand and gravel

Sand and gravel go into concrete, roads and foundations, and stay there. What a country uses decides what can ever come back.

Source: Eurostat, env_ac_mfa, 2022, kg per person a day.

How I worked this out

Domestic material consumption per person in 2022, divided by 365. Grass and fodder is fodder crops and grazed biomass. Limestone and other minerals covers every non-metallic mineral except sand and gravel. Every country is drawn on the same scale.

6 · What it's forMy calculation

More than half of Europe's material footprint ends up in buildings and food

Eurostat publishes this for the EU only.

Buildings are almost all investment, so whoever designs, specifies and builds decides how much material goes in. The manufactured goods households buy, from clothes to cars, come to about 6%.

Source: my grouping of Eurostat env_ac_rmefd, EU, 2022.

How I worked this out

The footprint counts the raw materials behind everything bought in the EU, including those extracted abroad for imports, and assigns them to 63 product groups, which I gathered into seven. Food includes hotels and restaurants.

Investment is new buildings, infrastructure, machinery and stocks, whoever pays, households buying new homes included. Spending is what households, governments and non-profits buy and use up.

7 · Which tonnes matter

Minerals are half the weight of what Europe uses, but a ninth of the climate impact

Published for the EU only.

A target in tonnes rewards recycling rubble. A carbon target rewards keeping metal in use and changing how food is grown.

Source: ETC CE Report 2025/3, Figure 3-4, EU, 2022.

How I worked this out

Read from Figure 3-4 of Measuring environmental benefits of circular economy (Vercalsteren and others, 2025), prepared for the European Environment Agency. The climate impact covers producing the materials, up to the factory gate, so burning fuel isn't included. Per tonne, metal ores carry the most.

So what

Using less has no ceiling

Recycling more of the little that comes back runs out at about Europe's target. Using less material doesn't. Most of that material goes into buildings and food, so the decisions sit with whoever designs, specifies, builds and buys.

Data and limits
  • The limits are bounds, not forecasts. They count every treated tonne as recyclable, contaminated soil and mining waste included, so real limits are lower.
  • They count only waste treated at home, so waste a country exports for energy recovery or disposal is left out.
  • Eurostat's published rate doesn't rebuild exactly from its own component tables, so every calculation starts from the published rate.
  • Ireland's strategy also quotes a 2.7% circularity rate from the Circularity Gap Report Ireland, a different measure. Its 12% target for 2030 is for the circular material use rate used here.
  • 2022 is the latest year where every figure is reported. Eurostat revised its 2022 waste figures on October 28, 2026, which lowered Hungary's limit by about 2 points and barely moved the rest. Its 2022 rates came out before its latest revision of consumption figures, so they may shift slightly at its next release.
