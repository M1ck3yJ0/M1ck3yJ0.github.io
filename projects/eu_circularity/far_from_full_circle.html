Far from Full Circle

Europe can't recycle its way to circularity

The EU wants 24% of its material to come from recycled waste by 2030, double today's share. I worked out the most recycling could ever deliver, for the EU and each of its 27 countries. Recycling every tonne of waste it has, Europe would only just get there.

By Milcah M. Joseph, November 2026. My calculation on Eurostat data for 2022.

Lowest ceiling first. Pick one to compare it with the EU.

Europe already recycles most of what it realistically can. Of the waste it treats but doesn't recycle, 71% is soil, dredged mud and mining rock. Everything else, recycled to the last tonne, would add four points.

Households generate 196 million tonnes of waste a year, 9% of all the waste Europe generates. Recycling every tonne of it would add at most 2.3 points, and less in practice, since some of it is recycled already.

Of every 100 tonnes of minerals, 67 are built into buildings, roads and machines. Much of that will return as demolition waste, decades from now. Fossil fuels and biomass are mostly burned or eaten, and never will. Recycling can only work with what comes back, and more than half of that is soil and rock.

Source: Eurostat env_ac_sd and env_wassd, 2022, rounded. The EU's bars leave out other products, about 1% of the total, and the soil and rock part is approximate. Waste here is Eurostat's flow measure, which leaves out soil and dredged mud sent to landfill and waste from treating waste: 1.67 billion tonnes, against the 1.97 billion tonnes of waste treated that the ceilings use.

Recycling every tonne, 15 of 27 countries would still fall short of 24%. Without soil and rock, all but three would.

Each country's rate in 2022 and how far recycling all of its waste would take it. Pick a country to compare it with the EU.

To reach 24% by recycling alone, a country needs about 32 tonnes of waste for every 100 tonnes of material it consumes. Fifteen don't have that much. The ceiling follows how much waste a country has, not how well it recycles. Italy recycles 86% of its waste and has almost nothing left to add, while nearly all of Romania's room is mining rock.

Source: my calculation on Eurostat env_ac_cur, env_ac_mfa and env_wastrt, 2022. Countries are grouped by the lower end of each range. Sweden doesn't publish how much of its waste is soil and rock.

What moved the rate in each two-year step, in points, with the net change as a black line

Each two-year step since 2010: recycling in blue, using more or less material in gray. From the least added by recycling to the most.

The biggest jump came from using less, not recycling more. From 2008 to 2010, as the financial crisis hit, Europe used 14% less material, and that alone added 1.3 points. Recycling more added 1.6 points from 2004 to 2010, and only 0.7 in the twelve years to 2022.

Eurostat's 2024 figures, still partly estimates, show it again: Europe used 6% less material than in 2022, and that alone added about 0.7 points. Two years of using less did as much as ten years of recycling more.

Source: my calculation on Eurostat env_ac_cur and env_ac_mfa, 2004 to 2024, with env_wastrt for the ceilings. Country rates start in 2010, and waste is reported every two years. 2024 values build on Eurostat's estimated rate.

Using less has no ceiling

Recycling more of the little that comes back runs out at about Europe's target. Using less material doesn't.

The four levers on one scale, with a line where each country's target sits. Least that recycling could add first.

Recycling's bars can't grow any longer. Using less can: with no more recycling, Europe would reach 24% using 59% less material, or 42% less if it also recycled all its waste but soil and rock.

That's less about shopping than it sounds: most of those tonnes go into buildings, roads, energy and food, and how much goes in is decided by whoever designs, specifies, builds and buys.

How much new material goes into what you make or build? How long will it last? Could the same job be done with less, or with what's already there?

How I worked this out, limits and sources

The ceiling

The circularity rate is recycled waste divided by recycled waste plus material consumption. Starting from Eurostat's published rate, I added every tonne of waste each country treated but didn't recycle. Holding consumption fixed gives the lower figure of each range. Letting each recycled tonne replace a tonne of new material gives the higher one.

Soil and rock means soils, dredging spoils and other mineral waste, mostly from mining. Rubble from construction and demolition stays in.

Where material goes

Eurostat's material flow accounts follow every tonne of material processed in a country: extracted at home, imported, recycled or backfilled. I took the shares built into stock, burned or eaten (emissions), exported, spread on land (dissipative flows) and treated as waste, and rounded them to whole tonnes out of 100. The soil and rock part of the waste is each country's soil and rock share of all the waste it treats, applied to the waste in the flows. Malta's flows record no emissions and leave about a fifth of its material unaccounted for.

The EU's bars split the same flows by material, which only the EU and 13 member states publish; other products, about 1% of the total, are left out. Eurostat splits dissipative flows across materials in the same shares in every country (60% metal ores, 25% biomass, 15% minerals), so the share of metal ores spread on land is an estimate. Waste in the flows leaves out soil and dredged mud sent to landfill and waste from treating waste, so it's smaller than the waste treated that the ceilings use.

What moved the rate

For each two-year step, I recalculated the rate with only recycling changing, once with the earlier year's consumption and once with the later year's, and averaged the two: that's the recycling effect. The rest of the change is the consumption effect, so the two add up to it.

What each lever is worth

Each lever changes one thing in 2022 and keeps the rest. The recycling levers recycle waste that was treated but not recycled, with consumption unchanged, the lower end of each range above. The others cut consumption with recycling unchanged: by 15%, by every tonne of fossil fuels, or by as much as it takes to reach the target. For household waste, I added all the waste households generated to recycling, as if none of it were recycled today, so it's an upper bound.

Limits

  • The ceilings are bounds, not forecasts. They count every treated tonne as recyclable, contaminated soil and mining waste included, so real ceilings are lower. Mining waste lifts the ceilings of Finland, Bulgaria, Romania and Sweden.
  • They count only waste treated at home, so waste a country exports for energy recovery or disposal is left out.
  • Consumption here is Eurostat's domestic measure. It counts grass grazed and ore mined in a country even when the beef or metal is exported.
  • Eurostat's published rate doesn't rebuild exactly from its own component tables, so every calculation starts from the published rate.
  • Ireland's strategy also quotes a 2.7% circularity rate from the Circularity Gap Report Ireland, a different measure. Its 12% target for 2030 is for the rate used here.
  • 2022 is the latest year where every figure is reported. Eurostat revised its 2022 waste figures on October 28, 2026, which lowered Hungary's ceiling by about 2 points and barely moved the rest.
